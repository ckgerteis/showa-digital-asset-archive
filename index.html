About the Archive

The Shōwa Digital Asset Archive is a project created and led by Dr Christopher Gerteis (SOAS University of London).

The Shōwa Digital Asset Archive is a collaborative hub for finding, checking and discussing 3D representations of Japan between 1926 and 1989. It holds no objects and no model files. It records where models, photogrammetric scans and digital reconstructions are published, who made them, the terms on which they may be used, and what each can show about the period. It grows through the listings, corrections and teaching materials that its users contribute.

Shōwa has become a style as much as a period. In Japan the current “Shōwa retro” boom took hold among young people around 2018, when photographs of cream soda in old-style kissaten spread across social media, according to the youth research unit SHIBUYA109 lab.; city pop records and bubble-era fashion followed. The boom has a precedent in Yamazaki Takashi’s film Always: Sunset on Third Street (2005), named Picture of the Year at the 2006 Japan Academy Film Prize.

Commercial leisure has followed the same turn. In May 2021 Seibuen Yūenchi, a struggling amusement park in Tokorozawa, reopened after a ten-billion-yen refit around Yūhi no Oka, a 130-metre shopping street that recreates the 1960s, with a Godzilla ride made with Yamazaki.

Abroad, the same years brought city pop to new listeners. A fan’s extended upload of Takeuchi Mariya’s “Plastic Love” (1985) circulated through YouTube’s recommendations from 2017. Matsubara Miki’s “Mayonaka no Door” (1979) spread from an Indonesian singer’s cover in 2020 onto global streaming charts and passed 500 million plays on Spotify in 2026.

The same appetite draws world builders and game designers to the period. Its material record is dense, recent and close at hand, and games such as Shenmue (1999), set in Yokosuka in 1986, and Yakuza 0 (2015), set in the Tokyo and Osaka of 1988, have built explorable districts from it. Nostalgia also smooths that record, selling the warmth of the shopping street while leaving out the overwork, pollution and coal that powered the boom. The archive pairs each asset with its provenance and its limits, so that a designer furnishing a 1960s danchi apartment and a student writing about postwar domestic labour can work from the same object. Courses can then teach recent history alongside game and virtual-world design, with each discipline testing the other’s claims.
